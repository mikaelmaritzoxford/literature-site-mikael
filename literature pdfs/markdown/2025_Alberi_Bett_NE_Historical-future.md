This learning will continue, but additional areas of focus around sustainable design and manufacturing will grow in importance. These advances must be intentionally pursued, as meaning- ful progress is unlikely to emerge from cumulative experience alone. In this Perspective we review recent progress in the development of PV technology and look ahead to identifying R&D activities that will be needed for this future. Cost reduction is expected to remain a critical driver of future progress, along with improvements in efficiency and reliability. A key component of future growth will also be ‘learning for sustainability’—that is, developing the knowledge, approaches and manufacturing ability to produce PV at multi-terawatt scale within the finite constraints of the Earth’s resources. We note at the outset that our assessment focuses on PV technology (that is, the module itself) and does not extend to other important aspects for achieving high levels of PV deployment, such as system design and pairing with storage to reduce generation variability.

### Historical ‘learning’ in PV

One way improvements have historically been examined is through the framework of the experience curve. The experience curve is an empirical mathematical concept that aims to capture how an industry

improves proficiency at a particular task as a function of cumulative experience. It is an effective analytical tool in many domains, including the cost of manufacturing, labour productivity and the reduction of material use 9–11. In the PV domain, it is often presented as price versus cumulative installations or time rather than manufacturing cost due to availability of historical data. We present the curve for single-junction PV modules in price (Fig. 1b), where reductions have been driven largely by silicon (Si) modules¹². The experience curve may be applied as long as the metric being analysed is improved by experience. The experience curve is not simply a model of economies of scale, although production volume learning is captured. In addition, the learning rate can evolve over time and is accelerated by sharing experience, particularly across the globe. Nota- bly, clusters of PV companies, first in the United States, then in Japan and Germany and today in China, have shared knowledge through the training of new employees, active collaboration and the cross-licensing of intellectual property. Regularization of wafer and module size has helped production tool manufacturers to lower development costs, and the implementation of software tools for simulating solar cells, modules and systems has accelerated PV system design. Broader adop- tion of smaller efforts has also influenced how the PV community works together, for example, US government support of the Jet Propulsion

Laboratory’s Block V reliability improvement program that shaped the current IEC 61215 qualification testing sequences¹³. R&D has also been central to advances captured in the experience curve, yet it is a separate factor from experience⁸. Notably, directed R&D is an additional and valuable form of learning that sets the foun- dation for improvements. New cell and module designs that lead to cost reductions are one example. As the needs of the industry shift, emphasis on where to focus R&D will follow suit. In this sense, we use the term ‘learning’ throughout this Perspective as a proxy for the complex set of factors, including R&D, that contribute to improvements across many metrics. Two other key performance parameters have also benefited from PV R&D coupled with experience: module efficiency and reliability. Module efficiency (Fig. 1c) is a key indicator of technical progress and benefits disproportionately from R&D breakthroughs. Its increase has hastened the economic viability of PV through decreases in the module area and corresponding balance of systems. Figure 1c also shows how record Si modules now approach the theoretical limit for Si single-junction technology 14,15. Likewise, module reliability has increased through the study of degradation mechanisms and continuous feedback from field perfor- mance. One indirect but readily available measure of reliability that reflects market expectations is the average number of years of module warranty (Fig. 1d) 16,17. We plot warranty as a function of cumulative global installation to indicate the role of experience, consistent with the common presentation of price reduction.

### The future of learning in PV

Drawing from this historical backdrop, we look towards future R&D needs in this new era for solar PV. We expect a reliance on continued progress in price, performance and reliability to drive PV installations. We also anticipate that additional factors will emerge to reach manu- facturing and deployment goals with available resources. This includes resource and material availability. We predict that net emissions, embodied energy, resource consumption and recycling will increas- ingly be considered in new designs and manufacturing approaches.

### Progress in historical learning for the near term

The future trajectory of the price/cost experience curve for PV modules is an interesting topic for debate. Even with continued manufacturing growth, the increments of cost decrease become smaller for every magnitude of additional deployment. As industries mature, cost mini- mization strategies are often increasingly coupled with or replaced by performance-maximizing strategies. Here we address the question of what advances in the near and longer terms will enable continued progress in PV performance and cost. Innovations in wafer production, cell and module design, manufacturing steps and balance-of-system components are traditional areas of focus for the PV industry. Improve- ments in module efficiencies, reliability, installation practices (for example, automation), engineering, permitting requirements and operations protocols are other cost and system performance drivers. With these advances, the PV industry has a demonstrated track record of surpassing earlier estimated ‘limits’ in module cost, performance and broader integration. We believe that there is more room to run as PV use expands worldwide. Emerging technologies, such as thin-film metal halide perovskites and tandem architectures, have also gained interest as potential ena- blers of future manufacturing-related cost reduction and efficiency gains, respectively. Several tandem architectures currently considered incorporate thin-film junctions into their designs to achieve both objec- tives. Substantial discussion has been devoted to the topic of tandem architectures as the next major step in the evolution of PV, addressing questions such as “what role will tandems play in the global distribution of PV installed by 2050 and beyond?” and “what learning is needed for tandems to be widely deployed?”.

The main advantage of tandem architectures is their potential boost in efficiency. Whereas the record Si module efficiency stands at 26.0%, module efficiencies are expected to peak at around 27% (refs. 14,18). Tandem technologies can surpass this limit, with small-area perovskite/Si cells and modules now demonstrated at 34.85 and 30.6%, respectively¹⁸. With sizeable investment, Si-based tandem modules may be expected to realize ~0.5% annual efficiency gains up to 2030, and are eventually predicted to reach >35% in the next decades 14,19. Higher efficiencies help to reduce the balance of systems and module cost per watt, if the relative watts gained exceed the relative increase in total cost and the degradation rates and warranties are similar to Si modules today. All-thin-film tandem architectures may enable manu- facturing with an even lower cost per watt, raw material consumption, capital expenditure and carbon intensity. Finally, innovations in tan- dem architectures and manufacturing may allow new industry players to enter the market, leading to a more globally diversified cell- and module-manufacturing landscape and supply chain. Despite their long-term efficiency potential, tandem solar cells (and in particular perovskite junctions) still require substantial improvements in reliability and scalability before they are ready for mass deployment. Manufacturing processes are also still being worked out. Tandem solar cells will undergo experience-based learning in all three areas, aided by previous lessons in Si, cadmium telluride (CdTe), CIGS (that is, copper indium gallium selenide) and III–V modules. Those technologies demonstrated the importance of R&D in addition to learning through cumulative production in unlocking their potential. Early adoption markets will also serve as valuable proving grounds. Questions remain about whether and when tandems will be competi- tive for mass deployment on module cost alone, or whether they can progress along the associated experience curve rapidly enough to meaningfully contribute to the buildout of manufacturing capacity for 3 TW yr −1 by 2050. A consensus view from the authors of this Perspective is that tandem architectures will be developed and deployed, given the balance-of-systems multiplier benefits associated with increased effi- ciency. Tandem architectures have the added advantage that they can sometimes leverage existing manufacturing infrastructure (particularly in the case of perovskite/Si tandems) to speed manufacturing scale-up. Collaboration across the global PV R&D community and a ramp-up in manufacturing are important for accelerating the pace of these advances. An increasing emphasis on new sustainability metrics may also shift the balance to those PV technologies that are best able to support them. Reliability plays a critical role in the economics and bankability of PV installations and the ability to meet global demand (that is, keeping modules in the field for longer reduces the need to install new ones). As with the case of cost, we may also ask “what is reliable enough?”, par- ticularly in the case of next-generation, very-high-efficiency modules. A prevailing view is that new modules will have to be at least as reliable as established ones to gain widespread adoption in terawatt-scale markets (although niche markets may be more accepting of higher degradation/ failure rates). New technologies must progress through four steps:

(1) specification of the module performance for controlled operat- ing conditions, (2) reaching a predictable short-term fielded energy output, (3) identification of the potential for early failure through standardized accelerated stress tests and (4) the mitigation of risks for new/unknown performance degradation modes. The fourth item remains an issue even for today’s Si technologies, as product evolution (for example, larger modules, thinner glass or evolving device designs) introduces new risk. Emerging perovskite and tandem technologies require atten- tion in all four measures. Extensive and transparent investigation to identify and understand degradation and failure modes, establish accelerated stress tests to probe those modes, and create strategies to mitigate them will help to make these technologies viable at scale. ### New learning for the next era of PV

What additional areas of focus for R&D, manufacturing innovation and collaboration are needed to sustain terawatt-scale PV? Energy, water and material-reduction strategies, combined with circular manufacturing approaches to further minimize the energy, carbon and waste footprints of PV, are all important aspects to consider. Energy sustainability at the system level, measured as an energy return on investment metric, in turn benefits from the underlying electricity produced by PV technologies 20–22. R&D focus on these aspects now will contribute to the long-term success of PV as a major energy source and a sustainable, profitable industry.

Silicon consumption per watt has come down eightfold in the past two decades (Fig. 2c), driven by reductions in wafer thickness and kerf loss, new methods for utilizing a greater fraction of the Si boule and increases in cell and module efficiencies 12,29. Usage per watt of panel power generation has greatly decreased over the past two decades, but the rate of future Ag reduc- tions is expected to slow. At the same time, anticipated future growth of Si module production will lead to an increase in overall consumption and the percentage of Ag supply utilized by the PV industry. Glass usage has likewise been reduced over the years³⁵. Whereas material reduction is generally positive from the standpoints of GHG emissions and energy consumption, it introduces design trade-offs. The recent move to larger Si modules coupled with the use of thin- ner glass coversheets (as low as 0.5 mm) has led to an increase in the mechanical failure rate of modules 35,36. Materials thrifting must be sup- ported with reliability research that is specific to climate zones, applica- tions and new module designs 37,38. Trends towards more widespread use of glass–glass module designs may also impact the trajectory of total glass usage in the future. Design for reliability, reuse and recycling represents a third route to enhanced sustainability³⁹. Increasing module lifetime is one route towards cost effectiveness and reduced emissions, as fewer modules must be manufactured as replacements over time. This is yet another benefit of learning for reliability. Designing for repair and reuse may have similar benefits, but this must not impair the module life or eco- nomic viability. Recycling is receiving increasing attention and can potentially become part of the supply chain⁴⁰. Although current vol- umes of decommissioned PV modules are not yet large enough, now is the time for R&D on module design for disassembly and recycling technologies. As the volume of decommissioned PV modules increases, materials science R&D, enhanced recycling process efficiencies, spe- cialization for local markets and improved logistics may also drive

down recycling costs and increase the amount of recovered material. Specific challenges include preventing the introduction of impurities into the recovered material, navigating an increasing bill of materials across a range of PV technologies and recovering high-value materials such as Si, metals and glass. These and other sustainability-focused metrics that emerge in the coming years will be important for the PV community to address head-on, as they are unlikely to be advanced through cumulative expe- rience alone. We predict that focused R&D efforts and collaboration will be concentrated in these areas going forwards. The improvements they yield will enable the manufacturing capacity and deployment of PV to continue to be scaled to the levels needed to meet new electricity demand.

### Outlook

Table 1 summarizes additional topics for learning in the relevant histori-

cal (cost, efficiency and reliability) and emerging (resource consump- tion, materials use and design for sustainability) categories. Learning for sustainability has already helped to drive down costs and will be even more critical for the PV industry to reliably access the resources needed for future deployment demands. However, some essential R&D activities may not yield immediate financial incentives for industry to take on alone. The entire PV com- munity must address these needs through continued R&D investment, shared experience, global collaboration and cooperation, standardi- zation and a healthy educational environment. New metrics are also

expected to emerge as PV fully transitions from an upstart technology to a major global energy source. Global redistribution of PV module manufacturing could further help to reduce supply-chain bottlenecks, improve access to PV and enhance its acceptance across more com- munities that directly benefit from its production and installation. The pace of learning in these categories will necessarily evolve throughout the transition to the multi-terawatt era of PV. Global instal- lations are proceeding along the trajectory previously projected by this group, in large part due to the investments made by the PV industry⁴. Maintaining this momentum will go a long way to reaching the 2050 goals. New goals set by the PV community will carry it into the future as a majority of its deployment objectives are met and manufacturing growth is no longer the main driver for industry. This mindset will be new for a community that has until now focused largely on growth and cost reduction. We must also not lose sight of the reason for advancing PV deploy- ment in the first place: securing an abundant, affordable, resilient and sustainable source of energy for humanity. Learning will also continue in the design of PV modules and systems to serve a wider range of com- munities, geographic and economic constraints and applications. Topics for future PV community meetings, such as the 4th Terawatt Workshop that informed this Perspective, may shift to addressing system and end-user needs. Investment, manufacturing and adoption today will pay globally transformative dividends tomorrow in terms of economic growth, productivity, job creation and reduced pollution and poverty. A focus on how the global PV community learns together will unlock this promising future.

### Methods

### Average Si module data

For the average Si module data presented in Fig. 1c, artificial intelli- gence was used to detect and classify tables in module datasheets that were provided as PDF (portable document format) files. The in-house developed tool ‘Lightning-table’ 41,42 for the end-to-end pipeline is divided into three major parts: (1) table-containing areas of the data- sheets are detected using deep-learning-based object detectors, (2) the raw values presented in the tables are extracted using off-the-shelf table extraction utilities and (3) the raw values are structured and validated using a combination of machine learning and rule-based methods. For (1), the library PyMuPDF⁴³ was used to convert the pages of the datasheets into images and the deep learning library PyTorch⁴⁴ together with object detector Faster R-CNN v2 45 was used to detect the tables in the images. For (2), the utilities Tabula⁴⁶ and Camelot 47,48

were used together with optical character recognition⁴⁹ to extract the data in the tables. For (3), the word vectorization technique known as term frequency-inverse document frequency (or TF-IDF) 50 and the naive Bayes⁵¹ classifier were implemented in Scikit-learn⁵² for structuring of the raw values. The complete pipeline achieved 99.9% precision and a 94.9% recall performance at extracting electrical, thermal and mechanical characteristics of tables in the datasheets from 2003 to 2023. The dataset for training and evaluation was compiled from data- sheets of PV modules obtained directly from manufacturers, either via their official websites or scanned from original printed materials. These datasheets span a period of approximately two decades. Data were sampled randomly from a larger pool of files, including 420 module datasheets from 140 manufacturers, resulting in a training dataset comprising 2,675 images. The total number of tables was 5,896 because most of the images contained multiple tables. Hyperparameters of the network were trained for 50 epochs, lasting about eight hours, with a batch size of four and a learning rate of 10 −4. Eighty per cent of the dataset was used for training and 20% of the dataset was used for evaluation. Owing to the age of many documents, the majority of the original web links are no longer active, and not all datasheets remain publicly accessible. Consequently, whereas some of the data may still

be found online, the full set of 420 datasheets from 140 manufacturers is no longer entirely available in the public domain.

### Calculation of cumulative displaced CO₂ data

The values for Fig. 2a were calculated by multiplying the relative con- tribution of PV energy to electricity generation *G*PV (given in per cent) with total global electricity generation *E*tot (given in terawatt hours), and multiplying that with the global average carbon intensity CItot̄ (given in grams per kilowatt hour). The resulting estimate is conserva- tive because industrially highly developed countries with high PV installation rates tend to have higher than average carbon emissions. Embedded carbon was estimated but only results in a minor correction (<3%) of the obtained value. Data for this calculation were taken from the following datasets: electricity generation by source from ref. 53 and carbon intensity from ref. 24. Electricity and water consumption data were obtained from pub- lished sustainability reports from the companies Trina, Jinko and First Solar. Because the total scope of the manufacturing processes included in these values may vary between reports from different companies, it is not advisable to directly compare values between them. The data were therefore averaged over all three companies.
