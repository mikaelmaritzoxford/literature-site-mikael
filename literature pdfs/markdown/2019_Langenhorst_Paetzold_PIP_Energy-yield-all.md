**1 INTRODUCTION**

Novel photovoltaic (PV) materials and concepts are of key importance to uphold the ongoing increase of record power conversion efficien- cies (PCEs) of PV.1,2One strategy to realize high PCEs are tandem solar cells, which reduce the charge carrier thermalization losses by harvesting high energy photons of the sun at a high voltage in the top solar cell and low energy photons in the low bandgap bottom solar cell (see Figure 1A). According to the detailed balance theory, the limit in PCE of tandem solar cells is approximately 46% and, thereby, much higher than the limit of single-junction (SJ) solar cells of approxi- mately 33%.3,4With the recent rise of perovskite PV, for the first time a low-cost, high efficiency, and wide bandgap absorber material is available that can be combined with the market-dominating PV tech-

nologies based on crystalline silicon (*E*g∼ 1.1eV) or thin-film copper indium gallium diselenide (CIGS;*E*g∼ 1.2 eV) to realize a high efficiency tandem solar module.5,6In 2015, the first perovskite/CIGS tandem solar cell was reported, reaching a remarkable PCE of up to 20.5%.7 Further optimizations led to a fast and substantial increase of the PCEs up to 23.9% for mechanically stacked tandem architectures8,9 and 22.4% for monolithic tandem architectures10on the cell level. Moreover, the first perovskite/CIGS tandem modules have been pro- totyped, reaching PCEs of up to 17.8%.11Next, to the high PCE, the technology offers the ease of an integrated all thin-film technology, which can be fabricated on thin, lightweight, and flexible substrates. Despite the fast advancement of the technology, further progress in PCE of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules has to be made such that the higher PCE of this technology justifies the higher complexity

compared with the SJ record PCEs of CIGS solar cells (22.9%)12and perovskite solar cells (23.3%).13As a consequence, perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules require optimization in order to reduce optical losses and maximize the power generation output.14-16To date, two types of architecture are researched and developed: A, mechanical stacking of perovskite top solar cell and low-bandgap bottom solar cell without direct electrical interconnection, called ‘‘four-terminal archi- tecture’’ (4T); and B, monolithic connection of the top and bottom solar cells in series, called ‘‘two-terminal architecture’’ (2T). The differ- ent architectures, illustrated in Figure 1B, involve distinct advantages and disadvantages. In the 4T architecture, the top and bottom solar cell can be operated at their individual maximum power point (MPP), but significant reflection and absorption losses are induced because of the requirement of thicker transparent electrodes for lateral charge transport.16In contrast, the 2T architecture exhibits thinner transpar- ent electrodes and, in turn, lower optical losses, but the monolithic interconnection of the top and bottom solar cell results in losses because of discrepancies in the currents of the subcells for vari- able irradiance spectra. Therefore, careful optimization of the annual energy yield (EY) under realistic solar irradiation conditions is required, in order to identify the realistic potential of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules and to elucidate the superiority of one architecture over the other. In the context of perovskite-on-silicon tandem solar cells, EY modelling is already used to perform optimizations of device architecture and to model the performance limitations.17-21 This work provides a systematic study on the EY of latitude-tilted (max. 45 ◦ ) perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules for various loca- tions in the United States of America. We employ optical simulations, combining the transfer-matrix method (TMM) and Beer-Lambert's law, for the accurate optical modelling of the perovskite/CIGS solar module device architecture. Combining these optical simulations with basic electrical simulations and detailed irradiance data, allows study- ing the EY of perovskite/CIGS solar modules in the 2T as well as 4T architecture for a typical meteorological year at exemplary locations in various climate zones. At this point, it shall be noted that we dis- regard temperature variations in this study for now as it focusses on

the optical modelling of perovskite/ CIGS tandem PV. Furthermore, we quantify the major loss mechanisms occurring in perovskite/CIGS solar modules, such as reflection, absorption, and current matching losses. Additionally, we investigate the impact of variations in spec- tral irradiance, in particular the average photon energy (APE), on the EY and optimum device architecture of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules. For these investigations, we choose methyl ammonium lead iodide (CH₃NH₃PbI₃) as the starting perovskite absorber material, since it is commonly used as a reference material and displays similar optical properties as the highest performing multi-cation perovskite absorbers. Nevertheless, the bandgap of metal-halide perovskites can be tuned arbitrarily in the range of 1.55 eV to 2.3 eV by compo- sitional variations of the halide in the perovskite crystal structure according to CH₃NH₃Pb(I1 − xBr*x*)3.22Therefore, we study the EY of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules in 2T and 4T architecture for artificial perovskite absorber materials of bandgaps ranging from 1.55 eV to 2.0 eV. It shall be noted that we disregard the complex key issue of long-term stability of perovskite PV at this stage, as this is beyond the scope of this paper which focuses on the optical aspects of perovskite/CIGS solar modules.

**2 METHOD: ENERGY YIELD MODELLING**

The flow of the EY modelling platform used in this study to determine the annual EY for the investigated architectures of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules is schematically depicted in Figure 2A. A more detailed description can also be found in a previous study.21In general, the EY modelling consists of four software modules that address the calculation of A, the irradiance based on meteorological data, B, the absorptance based on the device architecture, C, the electrical behavior based on electrical characteristics of the device, and D, the annual EY. The calculation of the irradiance relies on hourly and spectrally resolved typical meteorological year (TMY3) data and is available for a set of locations in the United States of America. Initially, clear sky irradiance with a specular and diffuse fraction is determined using

the simple model of the atmospheric radiative transfer of sunshine (SMARTS),24where the diffuse fraction is assumed to exhibit a Lam- bertian distribution over the whole sky. Subsequently, a simple model for the cloud coverage is applied that assumes irradiance originating from clouds to have the same spectral distribution as specular clear sky irradiance. The optical response of the investigated architectures is simulated using TMM for thin films that require a treatment of coherent forward and backward propagating electromagnetic waves. If ‘‘optically-thick’’ layers are apparent in the layer stack, a series expansion of the Beer-Lambert's law is sufficient to account for multiple reflections at the interfaces to adjacent layers. The optical calculation makes use of complex refractive indices and layer thicknesses of the refer- ence architectures.25,26For calculations with wide bandgap perovskite absorbers, optical data of mixed-halide perovskites available in a pre- vious study22is interpolated to obtain complex refractive indices with a bandgap resolution of 0.05 eV (see Figure S1). The architecture of the investigated tandem solar modules (Figure 1B) comprises of a combination of a perovskite top solar cell, a CIGS bottom solar cell as well as module specific layers, ie, an antire- flection coating (ARC), top cover glass, and an encapsulation material that we assume to be ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA). The perovskite solar cell consists of an indium tin oxide (ITO) front electrode, a titanium

dioxide (TiO₂) electron transport layer, the perovskite absorber itself, a 2,2,7,7-tetrakis(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD) hole transport layer, and a thin and thick transpar- ent ITO back electrode in the 2T and 4T architecture, respectively. Even though the low thermal stability of Spiro-OMeTAD hinders per- ovskite annealing in the 2T architecture,27we consider it as the hole transport material in the 2T architecture to maintain comparability between both architectures. Nevertheless, the choice of the hole transport material only has small impact on the optical performance, as can be seen from comparative simulations with 30 nm of nickel oxide (NiO) as hole transport material (see Figure S3). The selected perovskite absorber material is CH₃NH₃PbI₃ with a bandgap of 1.55 eV, since CH₃NH₃PbI₃ is a commonly used reference material in the field. Its optical properties are very similar to those of the recent most stable and highest performance perovskite solar cells employ multi-cation perovskites.28Additionally, we consider the transition between the perovskite absorber and the Spiro-OMeTAD as a mixed material consisting of two equal fractions of both materials. Thus, this mixed layer accounts for lower reflectivity because of the roughness of this interface. The improved match of the external quantum effi- ciency (EQE) of this approach with measurement results was already demonstrated in a previous study.26The front electrode of the CIGS cell consists of aluminum-doped zinc oxide (ZnO:Al) and intrinsic zinc oxide (i-ZnO). Furthermore, the CIGS cell involves a cadmium sulfide (CdS) buffer layer, the CIGS absorber layer, a molybdenum diselenide (MoSe₂) layer, and a molybdenum (Mo) back contact. It shall be noted that we disregard shadowing by electrical connections for all tandem architectures as well as the SJ references. We assume the architecture of the CIGS cell to be as in a previous study,25except disregarding the magnesium fluoride ARC. The 4T architecture requires an addi- tional intermediate insulating layer to separate both cells electrically. In earlier studies, air was commonly used as an intermediate layer.6,29 However, highly transparent polymers or silicones with higher refractive indices around 1.5, like commonly used EVA, should aid in reducing reflection losses because they enable a smoother transition of refractive indices between the top and bottom solar cell.30,31There- fore, we consider EVA as the intermediate layer in our simulations. We calibrate the optical models of both sub cells with regard to the EQE of the reference architectures of CIGS and opaque CH₃NH₃PbI₃ solar cells (as given in Figure 2B) by adjusting the layer thicknesses of all layers and the collection efficiencies of the absorbing layers. All layer thicknesses for both architectures used in this work are listed in Figure S2 and the sources of optical data are provided in Table S1. Combining, finally, the orientation of the tandem solar module, the course of the sun, the incident irradiance, the collection efficiency, and the layer-resolved absorption (see Figure S3), we calculate the generated photocurrent *J*SC. Subsequently, we determine the MPP of the current-density voltage (*J*-*V*) characteristic with the use of the single-diode equation. Thus, we obtain the power output for every hour of the year. With regard to the electrical properties of various wide bandgap perovskite materials, we assume a comparable electrical performance of the perovskite top solar cells by maintaining a fill factor (FF) of 80% under an illumination of air-mass 1.5 global (AM1.5g) solar irradiation for all bandgaps (see Table S2). Furthermore, for each bandgap, we calculate the dark saturation current density *J₀* based on

the black-body radiation at a temperature of 300 K and the assumption of a perfect EQE.19This approach results in a linear increase of the open-circuit voltage (*V*OC) with increasing bandgap (see Figure S4), which is in agreement with similar studies.19 Based on the above-outlined method, we carry out optimizations of the device architecture of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules in 2T and 4T architecture with the objective to quantify reflection, parasitic absorption, and current matching losses. Furthermore, we determine the dependency of the perovskite absorber thickness and the perovskite bandgap on variations in spectral irradiance. The selec- tion of locations intends to cover various classes of the Köppen climate classification.32A list of all considered locations and their correspond- ing Köppen climate classification is provided in Table S2. Therefore, we perform EY calculations for a set of locations in the United States of America, covering different climate zones.

**3 RESULTS**

First, we compare the overall EY of latitude-tilted perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules in 2T and 4T architecture with the reference CIGS SJ solar module for various locations in the United States of America, covering a broad range of climate zones (see Figure 3A). For each location, the 2T architecture requires an optimization of the perovskite absorber thickness such that losses arising from mismatch in the current density of the perovskite top solar cell and the CIGS bottom solar cell are minimized. It should be kept in mind that the choice of the charge transport material has a small impact on the

optical performance layers in all thin-film perovskite/ CIGS tandem PV (see Figure S3). Consequently, to minimize current matching losses in the 2T architecture, the perovskite layer thickness should be optimized for each layer stack individually. For all considered locations, the perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules significantly outperform the reference CIGS SJ solar module in terms of EY by 31%-36% and 35%- 41% for the 2T and 4T architec- tures, respectively (see Figure 3B). Also, in terms of PCE in Figure S5, determined under standard test conditions (STC), we find a relative increase of approximately 32% for the 2T architecture (absolute PCE = 25.7%, PCEtop= 16.1%, PCEbottom= 9.6%) and a relative increase of approximately 30% for the 4T architecture (absolute PCE = 25.3%, PCEtop= 17.8%, PCEbottom= 7.5%) compared with reference CIGS SJ solar modules (absolute PCE = 19.5%). Thereby, our EY study demon- strates the promise of perovskite/CIGS tandem PV as a future high performance and all thin-film PV technology. Moreover, we identify a relative increase in PCE of 35% for the 2T architecture and of 32% for the 4T architecture compared with an opaque CH₃NH₃PbI₃ SJ solar module (absolute PCE = 19.1%). Despite having a lower absolute PCE, the opaque CH₃NH₃PbI₃ SJ solar module exhibits a higher EY in all investigated locations by 6.9% in average compared with the reference CIGS SJ solar module. Furthermore, the EY of the 4T architecture for a tandem module based on CH₃NH₃PbI₃ is higher than the EY of the 2T architecture (around 3.5% relative) for all locations, whereas the PCE shows the reverse trend. Comparing the EY of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules at various locations, the EY in hot and sunny regions (Köppen climate classification: Tropical and Arid) in the southwest (Phoenix, Arizona) is higher than in cold and damp climatic regions (Köppen climate classification: Temperate and Boreal) in the north (Portland, Oregon) according to expectations. It is worth noting that the relative increase of EY of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules compared with CIGS SJ solar modules correlates roughly with the APE of the average irradiation spectrum of each location (see Figure 3B and Table S2). We attribute this correlation to higher thermalization losses of the CIGS SJ solar module in locations with higher APE, which implies a larger contribution of photons of short wavelengths to the irradiance spectra. The realistic irradiation conditions considered in the EY modelling differ from the STC by variations of: A, the solar irradiation spectra; B, the angle of incidence of the specular solar irradiation; and C, the contribution of diffuse solar irradiation. In order to discriminate the impact of these variations on the EY of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules, we derive three artificial irradiation scenarios from the realistic irradiation conditions in Phoenix, Arizona. The considered irradiation scenarios are: A, *Phoenix Real Spectra*, representing the real irradiance data; B, *Phoenix Invariant Spectra*, disregarding spectral variations by employing the AM1.5g spectrum normalized to the real irradiance intensities; C, *Phoenix all I*specular, assuming all solar irradiation is specular; and D, *Phoenix all I*diffuse, assuming all solar irradiation is diffuse (see Figure 4). Comparing the EY of perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules for these solar irradiation scenarios, it is apparent that the relative differences in EY between the 2T architecture and 4T architecture for *Phoenix all I*specularand *Phoenix all I*diffusemerely vary from the reference *Phoenix Real Spectra*. In contrast, the relative difference in EY between the 2T and 4T architecture is lower and even reverses in sign for *Phoenix Invariant Spectra*, which agrees also with the trend of higher PCE under STC for the 2T architecture compared with the 4T architecture. Thereby, we demonstrate that variations of irradiation spectra in the reference *Phoenix Real Spectra* are the major cause of the lower EY enhancement for the 2T architecture compared with the 4T architecture. We can further conclude that the variations in the angle of incidence as well as the ratio of specular and diffuse irradiation are both of much lower relevance. From a device perspective, these findings imply that the variations of the spectra in the realistic irradiation scenarios impact significantly on the current matching in the 2T architecture. In some locations, the 2T architecture outperforms the 4T architecture by a few percent and in others vice versa. In gen- eral, the difference in EY provided by both architectures is vanishing. In view of maximizing the prospects of the technology, these results clearly underline the need for highly efficient wide bandgap perovskite absorber materials for the use in tandem configurations. At this point it is worth noting that yet such designed materials, as investigated in this study, do not exist. Wide bandgap mixed halide perovskites typi- cally exhibit a FF *<*80%36and suffer from a high deficit in open-circuit voltage due to photo-induced phase segregation.37 Furthermore, our study reveals that the optimum perovskite bandgap for perovskite/CIGS tandem solar modules varies for realistic irradiation conditions in the range of 1.8 eV to 1.85 eV. For the 2T architecture, the optimum perovskite bandgap differs for various loca- tions, eg, 1.85 eV in Phoenix (APE = 1.51 eV) and 1.8 eV in Portland (APE = 1.49 eV), since the APE of the investigated location affects the amount of current matching losses. Contrary to that, the 4T archi- tecture does not display a dependence on the investigated location. In addition, the optimum perovskite bandgap is moderately higher for the 2T architecture, due to its dependence on current matching, than for the 4T architecture. When limiting the perovskite absorber thickness to more common thicknesses of 500 nm, the same optimum bandgap of 1.8 eV is identified (see Figure S9). These aspects accen- tuate the fact that the 4T architecture is more robust with regard to variations in spectral irradiation, whereas the 2T architecture promises a comparable EY if optimized appropriately.

**CONCLUSION**

In this work, we present EY modelling of perovskite/ CIGS tandem solar modules for various locations covering a broad range of climate zones.

Compared with reference CIGS SJ solar modules, CH₃NH₃PbI₃/CIGS tandem solar modules with parameters taken from real state-of-the-art devices show a remarkable relative increase in EY of up to 41%, which highlights the great promises of this all thin-film technology. Comparing the 2T and 4T architecture, the former features a higher PCE under STC but a lower EY under realistic conditions. Our study identifies the spectral variations in realistic irradiation conditions as the main cause for the lower EY of the 2T architecture, despite featuring lower reflection and parasitic absorption losses compared with the 4T architecture. As a consequence, even the EY of optimized 2T device architectures is lower by 3.5% in average compared with the 4T architecture throughout all investigated locations. One route to minimize the dependence of the 2T architecture on current matching and to enhance the overall performance of the tech- nology is to optimize the bandgap of the perovskite top solar cell. In fact, our study, based on artificial wide-bandgap perovskite materi- als, shows a comparable EY for the 2T architecture with optimized perovskite absorber layers in the bandgap range of 1.75 eV to 1.85 eV to the 4T architecture. The relative increase in EY for bandgaps around 1.8 eV can be improved further to remarkable values of up to 52%. These findings highlight the importance of recent research on developing high efficiency wide bandgap perovskites for tandem PV.
