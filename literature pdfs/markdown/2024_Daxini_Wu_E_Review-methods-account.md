# Review of methods to account for the solar spectral influence on photovoltaic device performance

Rajiv Daxini ∗, Yupeng Wu *Faculty of Engineering, University of Nottingham, Nottingham, NG7 2RD, United Kingdom*

A R T I C L E I N F O A B S T R A C T

*Keywords:* The accuracy of photovoltaic (PV) performance forecasts is essential for improving grid penetration, fault Spectrum detection, and financing of new installations. Failing to account for the spectral influence on PV performance Spectral correction can lead to weekly errors of up to 14% even for relatively stable technologies such as polycrystalline silicon. Photovoltaic There exist a range models, known as spectral correction functions (SCFs), to account for the spectral influence Short-circuit current on PV performance forecasts. These SCFs use different methods to characterise both the shift in PV performance Performance modelling Mismatch due to the spectrum, and the solar spectrum itself. This review analyses the merits and limitations of seven commonly used spectral characterisation indices — five proxy variables (air mass, clearness index, precipitable water, aerosol, diffuse solar radiation ratio) and two variables extracted from the spectral distribution (average photon energy, depth of a water absorption band). The same analytical approach is adopted to review a further four indices (mismatch factor and its variants, (weighted) useful fraction, normalised short-circuit current) that are commonly used to characterise the variation in PV performance due to the solar spectrum. A review of ten SCFs that are based on these indices is undertaken to analyse the current state of the art of spectral correction modelling. The results of the review show that whereas some proxy-variable methods offer a simple and convenient way to account for the spectral influence in PV performance forecasts, they are surpassed in terms of accuracy by SCFs based on parameters derived directly from the spectrum, such as the average photon energy and the depth of spectral absorption bands. A decision-making framework is proposed to guide PV performance modellers in their choice of spectral correction model. The framework considers system specifications, climate, data availability, etc. The results of this work may be applied in, for example, software packages for PV performance forecasting to enable more accurate case-specific power forecasts. In future work, a standardised comparison of all SCFs and their respective indices is necessary to quantify the differences between a wider range of models than is currently available in the literature and substantiate the proposed framework.

*2.1. Spectral irradiance and the spectral response* Spectral irradiance (SI) is defined as energy incident on a horizontal unit area per unit time per unit wavelength, in W m−2nm−1. The solar spectrum contains information on the distribution of irradiance across different wavelengths. Terrestrial spectral irradiance differs from that which is emitted by the sun due to two phenomena: (1) **Scattering** by air molecules, water, and dust; (2) **Absorption** by ozone (shorter wavelengths) and water vapour and carbon dioxide (longer wave- lengths) [47–49]. The effects of atmospheric scattering and absorption are illustrated inFig.3, which shows the extraterrestrial solar spectrum and the reference air mass (AM) 1.5 spectrum [50]. Spectral characterisation* The different response ranges and peaks at different wavelengths for the various PV technologies means that the way in which the different atmospheric components interact with the spectrum, the ef- fects of which are shown inFig.3, can have differing impacts on the performance of different PV technologies. These different atmospheric parameters, and their effects on PV performance and the solar spec- trum, are discussed in this section. In order to validate the relia- bility of the APE parameter to represent solar spectral distributions, Minemoto et al. adopt the IEC’s methodology for rating the spectral matching of a solar simulator [107] and find that a single APE value yields a spectral irradiance distribution with a relatively small standard deviation [108]. They conclude that the APE is a bijective index that can uniquely represent different solar spectral distributions. The APE parameter has been used extensively in subsequent research to characterise the spectral irradiance distribution [109–113]. Unlike the proxy variable methods, rather than taking one variable that affects the spectrum to represent it, the APE is extracted directly from the spectrum after all possible factors have already influenced it. Therefore, it is argued widely in the literature that the APE contains information on the dominant environmental conditions influencing PV performance and is therefore capable of effectively characterising the shape of the incident spectral distribution [114–116]. Adopting the same methodology as Minemoto et al. but analysing the spectra using the coefficient of variation (CV) rather than the standard deviation, Nofuentes et al. argue that the APE is in fact not a unique characteristic of the spectral distribution [117]. They find that in the 450–900 nm waveband, the CV remains below 3.3%, which is acceptable, but outside of this waveband the CV reaches values of up to 5%–11%. Between 350–450 nm, the CV lies around 5%–6%, and reaches 6%–11% for the 900–1050 nm waveband. They conclude that the APE may only be considered approximately unique within this mid-range waveband of 450–900 nm for the climate considered in their study, but otherwise the APE is not a unique characteristic of the spectrum. They attribute the spread about the mean predominantly to experimental uncertainty for wavelengths below 450 nm, which could be mitigated in future work, but attribute it to a mixture of experimental uncertainty and the direct effects of aerosols and water vapour for wavelengths longer than 900 nm. Much of the existing PV-APE research [118–120] tends to focus on thin-film PV devices, in particular aSi technologies. Comparison of SCFs** The previous sections show the high level of diversity in method- ologies used to model the spectral influence on PV performance and incorporate this influence into forecasts of PV performance. A summary of these mehtods is presented inTable10. In this section, a critical comparison is made between the different methods in terms of their un- derlying principles, such as which parameters are used to characterise the spectrum, and the differences in their predictive performance. Starting with *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎*), this is the most widely-known SCF and the model coefficients have been published for dozens of PV technologies from a range of manufacturers. The free and ready availability of model coefficients make *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎*) and obvious choice for PV performance mod- ellers. The ease with which *𝐴𝑀𝑎*can be calculated accurately in the field, without the need for specialist equipment and measurements, also makes deriving new coefficients for novel technologies relatively easy. However, air mass is defined by the path length of direct irradiance only, hence spectral effects linked to diffuse irradiance are omitted. Zanesco and Krenzinger [160] highlight that spectral variations are due not only to air mass, but also atmospheric precipitable water, Angstrom’s turbidity coefficient, and aerosol particle size distribution. In environments where only the effects of air mass dominate, *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎*) may sufficiently capture the majority of the spectral effects on PV performance [140]. However, Hansen et al. [161] find a systematic error in the air mass function where, at the same air mass, the spectral effects on *𝐼*scvary depending on the time of day. Klise et al. [162] attribute uncertainty in Sandia Model PV performance predictions, which use *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎*), in different geographic locations, to the effect water vapour content and atmospheric turbidity on the spectral distribution.
With all SCFs, quantification of their uncertainties for comparison is difficult due to the differences in the methodologies and datasets used to derive them. Elements of comparison using standardised methods exist in some publications, typically when new models are compared to existing models. In addition, some indication of relative performance may be gleaned from analysing model fitting statistics in the context of the methods through which they were derived. In their proposal of *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎,𝑊*), Lee and Panchula [147] compare linear regression results of outdoor testing data for *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎*), *𝑓* (*𝑊*), and *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎,𝑊*). **6. Discussion and recommendations for further work** Although there seems to be a consensus on the choice of meth- ods to characterise the variation in PV performance due to variation in the spectrum, with most studies adopting the mismatch factor or normalised short-circuit current, with a few variants thereof, there is great debate over the choice of spectral characterisation method. One of the likely causes of this range of methods being promoted is the lack of standardised testing for the different methods, particularly in different locations, climates, and for different PV systems. Although many individual studies offer comparisons between different SCFs, the methodologies between studies are disparate, yielding intercom- parisons of a wider range of SCFs for application in and to a wider range of climates and PV technologies practically impossible. However, this review finds that, overall, the available literature does seem to suggest that the direct spectral characterisation methods are likely to be more accurate than the proxy variable methods in most situations. However, spectral irradiance measurements are not currently widely utilised at PV plants and test sites. Therefore, the most accurate model may not be the most suitable for the end user. For example, in the absence of spectral irradiance data, be it simulated or measured, *𝑓* (*𝜑*) would be inappropriate. In another scenario where the test site typically experiences cloudless skies but has high levels of atmospheric aerosol pollution, *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎,𝐴𝑂𝐷,𝑊*) might be a better choice than *𝑓* (*𝐴𝑀𝑎,𝐾𝑡*). For example, no distinction is made different types of data, namely simulated and measured, as a comparison between the resultant model accuracies using these different types of data have not been analysed in the literature. If the research gaps highlighted in this section are addressed, a more detailed and precise framework could be developed. though a plethora of different methods exist to model the spectral influence on PV performance, these methods have been derived and validated using entirely different methodologies. The key differences in the methodologies include the data acquisition methods, data process- ing and filtering conditions, the type of PV devices investigated, the climate region under which the investigation is undertaken, and the seasons of the year during which the investigations are undertaken. These differences render the results presented for different models incomparable. Therefore, standardised measurement campaign and data analysis protocols should be deployed in a range of different climate regions for a set of the same, or at least comparable, PV modules. The imme- diate benefit of such a campaign would be the possibility to compare reliably and accurately the performance of different spectral correction functions. Not only would these results indicate the relative accuracies of the different models, but also enable the determination of their best use case scenarios. The second recommendation for future work is on the subject of developing specific spectral correction models. The models based on parameters derived directly from the spectrum, such as APE and *𝜀*, rather than proxy variables, such as *𝐴𝑀* and *𝑊*, are more accurate. *𝑎* Despite their lower accuracy, proxy variables can easily be calculated based on time of day and other routine and cheap measurements of variables such as temperature and humidity. While spectrometers are falling in price [170], they are still relatively expensive in comparison with other meteorological sensors. Spectral irradiance data are not typ- ically measured at PV test sites and power plants either. Therefore, the spectrum-based SCFs suffer a limitation in terms of the availability of their required input parameters. Nevertheless, many spectral simulation models exist [59,111,171,172] and, in more recent years, have been developed to include the effects of more complex weather conditions such as cloud cover [173]. The benefit of spectral-based models is clear, but these models that have used empirical spectral irradiance data should be validated with simulated spectral irradiance data. If such models can maintain their high level of accuracy with simulated input spectra, their usability and application potential would be enhanced greatly. If the two research gaps highlighted in this section are met, another review of published SCFs, using standardised results and both measured and simulated input parameters, would be useful. The results of such a review could guide PV performance modellers in their selection of models based on PV system specifications, climate and geography, forecasting frequency required, availability of data and whether it is measured or simulated, and model complexity.

**7. Conclusion** Models for the solar spectral influence on PV performance are a crucial component of PV performance models. These models apply a correction to a reference performance value to account for the influence of the spectrum, and are hence known as spectral correction functions (SCFs). Currently, a wide range of different SCFs exist. Some of these models use input parameters derived directly from the spectrum, while others use proxy variables to represent the solar spectrum. This review has shown that the proxy variable SCFs are a mature type of model and offer a relatively easy way to account for the spectral influence on PV performance. However, the applications may be more case-specific than currently evident in the literature, due to the limited range of information the dependent variables contain on the shape of the solar spectrum. Other models whose input parameters are derived directly from the solar spectrum, such as the average photon energy and the depth of water absorption bands or atmospheric windows, offer a more widely applicable solution to spectral correction modelling. However, the simulation of such spectral parameters is more complex than that of the proxy variable parameters. The published spectral parameter-based SCFs have not been validated using simulated inputs. A standardised measurement campaign is necessary to compare the relative accuracies of the different SCFs and determine when and where each model is the optimal choice. Key areas for investigation through this kind of standardised campaign are the performance of different models in specific climate conditions and for different PV devices. In addition, a comparison between model performance based on the use of both measured and simulated data is necessary, in particular for spectrum-based SCFs such as *𝑓* (*𝜑*) and *𝑓* (*𝜑,𝜀*). A decision-making framework for choosing the optimal SCF based on different use case scenarios has been hypothesised based on the anal- ysis in this review. This framework could be validated and developed further based on the results of a standardised investigation as described in this study.
