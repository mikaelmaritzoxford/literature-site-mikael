Additionally, optimization revealed that thinner silicon bottom cells require a higher perovskite top cell bandgap, impacting the balance between fabrication cost and cell stability.

## 1. Introduction

Monolithic tandem solar cells have emerged as a highly promising approach, with certified efficiencies surpassing the crystalline silicon (c- Si) record [1–3]. Featuring two-junction structures and an ideal pairing of top and bottom sub-cells, the theoretical power conversion efficiency (PCE) can exceed 40 % [4]. Among the various two-junction tandem technologies, the perovskite/silicon tandem has shown great promise, achieving efficiencies of 34.2 % in monolithic [5] (i.e., two-terminal) configurations. Additionally, perovskite-based tandem solar cells have garnered significant research interest due to their potential to improve the PCE at reasonable costs while maintaining compatibility with existing silicon technology [6,7]. When optimizing tandem solar cells, a key question is whether their efficiency, measured in-house under standard test conditions (STC),

Typically, the efficiency of a solar cell is quantified by its maximum output power under a standard solar spectrum of air mass (AM) 1.5, and temperature of 25◦C, and an irradiance of 100 mW/cm 2. However, the actual solar spectrum varies significantly with location, time, and weather. Addi tionally, diffuse light, which is predominant on cloudy days and differs from the STC spectrum, impacts the panels at various angles, along with the direct spectrum [8]. A critical factor in optimizing monolithic tan dem solar cells is ensuring current matching between the two subcells to achieve maximum PCE [9,10]. Changes in the spectrum, incident angle of incident light, and temperature can lead to current mismatches, potentially reducing the cell’s PCE. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate solar cells based on energy yield in response to real-world solar irradi ance, rather than solely relying on STC measurements. Besides the bandgap, thickness optimi zation is also an important factor, as it can lead to decreases in manufacturing costs, especially for silicon substrates with thicknesses below the industry standard of 130 μm. It has been concluded that the difference in optimal absorber thicknesses between the maximum annual EY and the maximum PCE under STC can be as much as 30 % for multi-junction solar cells, such as monolithic perovskite/CIGS [13] and perovskite/silicon [14] cells. As for monolithic perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells, energy yield (EY) simulations have been conducted with varying degrees of complexity depending how parameters such as irradiance [15], tandem device ar chitectures [16–21], temperature [22,23], and tracking systems [24, 25], are taken into account. The primary goals of the models included predicting and optimizing EY across different geographical locations, comparing the performance of 2-terminal and 4-terminal tandem de vices, enhancing light management by examining the effects of textured surfaces, qualitatively and quantitatively studying the impact of light-soaking recovery on tandem perovskite-based devices [26], or comparing the performance of bifacial silicon-heterojunction cell and 2-terminal tandem devices [27]. However, the interaction between sub-cells in the tandem structure was not considered in these EY models. In fact, luminescent coupling (LC) – the reabsorption of luminescent photons emitted by the high-bandgap top cell into the low-bandgap bottom cell – is an inherent phenomenon of tandem cells that occurs naturally within the monolithic tandem structure [28,29]. Bowman et al. highlighted the capability of LC in enhancing flexibility in subcell thicknesses and tolerance to different spectral conditions [30]. Howev er, the effects of LC on the optimization of solar cell structures under real working conditions have been overlooked in recent simulation models for perovskite based tandem solar cells. Although LC was considered for the first time in calculation of the energy output of perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells, it was calculated from the optical model overlooking the effect of electrical factors and assuming it is constant with the change of outdoor working conditions [31]. In fact, LC efficiency is strongly dependent on excitation conditions and on time and location under outdoor operation [32–35]. Therefore, a notable gap in the literature is the lack of comprehensive EY models for monolithic perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells that consider the effects of sub-cell interactions using experimental LC efficiency and its varia tion with the working environment on output power and structure optimization. In this study, we introduce a comprehensive optical device model used to simulate perovskite-on-silicon tandem solar cells, integrating real weather data. This model predicts the annual energy yield at spe cific locations in the northern hemisphere, considering various influ encing factors. For the first time, we explore the combined effects of sub- cell interaction using experimental LC efficiency and its dependence on working conditions, along with other factors such as variations in radiative recombination with irradiance intensity, temperature effects on absorptivity spectra, and cloud cover conditions. Initially, we calculated the influence of temperature on absorptivity, assuming changes in the bandgap of silicon and perovskite materials with the operating temperature of the photovoltaic (PV) module. Subsequently, we assessed LC efficiency based on the ratio of re-emitted photons escaping from both the rear and front sides of the perovskite top cell, derived experimentally from our previous work [36]. This provides a

more realistic estimate of the LC effect. As a result, we observed energy yield improvements of up to 1.4 % with LC, based on EY calculations from Typical Meteorological Year (TMY) data in Daggett, USA, using current records for radiative recombination in perovskite material. This improvement could reach up to 4 % with the direct bandgap material record. Since radiative recombination scales with the excitation in tensity, we also evaluated the impact of variations in LC efficiency and radiative recombination with the working conditions on output power by estimating this at each level of solar input. We found that these variations overall reduce the energy yield due to the decrease in light intensity during the daytime compared to standard conditions, which negatively impacts the LC effect and the efficiency of the tandem cell. We optimized the tandem cell structure by varying three main factors: the thickness of the perovskite top cell, the thickness of the silicon bottom cell, and the bandgap of the perovskite top cell, tailoring these optimizations to each specific location.

## 2. Methodology

The energy yield of the monolithic tandem solar cell was calculated under different scenarios using various models. The irradiance module calculated the spectrally and angularly resolved direct and diffuse irradiance spectra based on typical meteorological year (TMY) data from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) [37]. This was achieved by combining a simple model of atmospheric radiative transfer of sunshine (SMARTS) and a cloud model to produce the desired hourly-resolved irradiance from the TMY3 data [38,39].

In such tandem structures, if the recombination is radiative, the light re-emitted from the top cell can be absorbed and utilized by the bottom cell, a phenomenon known as LC. Specifically, in perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells, only the light emitted by the perovskite top cell is considered because it can be absorbed by the silicon bottom cell. The silicon cell, being an indirect bandgap semiconductor, has a very low rate of radiative recombination, and its emission is therefore neglected in our model. Moreover, the energy of photons emitted by silicon closely matches its own bandgap energy, rendering them unsuitable for ab sorption by the perovskite top cell, which has a larger bandgap than silicon. Despite the direct bandgap properties of metal halide perovskite semiconductors, initial reports of external electroluminescence quantum efficiency (ELQE) for perovskite solar cells were notably low, approxi mately ≈ 104% [42].

### 3.2. Effect of radiative recombination

Moreover, the difference in EY with and without the LC effect exceeded 1 % when considering the current record for radiative recombination in perovskite single junction solar cells (8.4 %, Fig. 2a). Therefore, enhancing radiative recombination also amplified the LC effect. Additionally, with lower radiative recombination, the optimal thickness for the perovskite top subcell tended to decrease since the reabsorption of photons by the bottom cell is insufficient to balance the

mismatch between the subcells. For instance, the optimal thickness of the perovskite layer for a 250 μm silicon bottom subcell changes from 550 nm to 450 nm when the ELQE drops from 25 % to 8.4 % (Fig. 2b). However, if the thickness of the silicon bottom cell is less than 250 μm, the optimal thickness for the perovskite top cell remains unchanged under the effect of LC due to the reduced probability of being reabsorbed in the silicon bottom cell. Most recent modelling has assumed a constant ELQE with the exci tation intensity. This assumption causes an overestimate in the calcu lated output power. In the ideal case of purely radiative recombination, the ELQE is independent of illumination intensity. However, most recent studies have shown an intensity dependence according to the power law PL(I) ∝ I k, with k being the power-law parameter and I the incident excitation intensity [32–35]. To understand the effect of ELQE varia tions on the output power, we compared the EY calculated from TMY data at Daggett, USA, using a constant ELQE and an ELQE that changes with a power of 0.6, as observed in a recent study [32]. We observed a decrease in EY when comparing scenarios of constant radiative recom bination to those with varying radiative recombination as excitation intensity changed, both in the presence and absence of the LC effect. However, the decrease was less pronounced under the LC effect (Fig. 3a–b). ### 3.3. Effect of bandgap

The tunability of bandgap energy is a key attraction for tandem ap plications, making bandgap optimization as crucial as the thickness of absorbers. In monolithic tandem structures, a higher bandgap of the top cell could yield a higher EY with less need for a thick bottom absorber. However, a higher perovskite bandgap could also lead to instability in the top cell performance [47,48]. Therefore, rather than recommending specific values for bandgap and absorber thicknesses, we optimized the absorber thickness for each bandgap value to achieve the highest EY. The results show that the LC effect not only enhances output power but also reduces the necessary thickness of absorbers and the bandgap of the perovskite top cell. For example, with no LC, the bandgap of the perovskite needs to be at least above 1.7 eV to reach the highest EY values. However, this requirement drops to less than 1.68 eV when considering the LC effect (Fig. 4a–d). Interestingly, the requirement for the bandgap remains low even when the silicon bottom cell thickness is reduced to 250 μm with the LC effect, while a bandgap above 1.7 eV is required for all different thicknesses of the silicon subcell if the LC effect is not considered. Additionally, at thin absorber levels (300 nm perov skite and 100 μm silicon), the EY is quite low, but it peaks at a low perovskite bandgap of 1.60 eV. As the absorber thickness increased, the EY also increased, reaching its peak at a higher bandgap of the perov skite top cell (Fig. 4e). Interestingly, we found a slightly different bandgap dependence when optimizing monolithic tandems for energy yield across different locations. We observed that in Denver, the peak in performance

appeared to shift towards a lower bandgap of the perovskite cell, which could be explained by the relative redshift of the solar spectrum (Fig. S1). For example, the optimized bandgap for a 100 μm silicon bottom cell is 1.66 eV (with LC effect) and 1.68 eV (without LC effect), instead of the requirement of 1.72 eV in Daggett. Interestingly, the maximum EY is achieved with a bandgap of 1.64 eV, which is much lower compared to the standard condition (Fig. 5a–d). Additionally, the optimized thickness of the perovskite is thicker than that in the previous location since the irradiation shifted to the low wavelength range, and the perovskite top cell needed to be thicker to effectively absorb those photons. For example, the optimization for the perovskite thickness had to be greater than 800 nm for a silicon bottom cell thickness greater than 100 μm, whereas previously it was only 450 nm (Figure S3a and Fig. 1a). Moreover, the irradiance shift could be reflected in the current mismatch between subcells as shown in Figure S3b. It is noteworthy that this trend is similar to that observed in Daggett but requires a thicker perovskite top cell (Figure S3c).

## 4. Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a comprehensive EY model that encompasses the full range of potential factors on the output power of a monolithic tandem device. Each factor plays a distinct role in shaping the device’s performance. For instance, variations in radiative recom bination with excitation intensity, if not accounted for, typically lead to an overestimation of the EY. Greater variations in the intensity of inci dent light correspond to more significant losses in the device’s output power in practical applications. Conversely, the LC effect positively impacts the EY by efficiently recycling excess photons from the top cell to the bottom cell. Consequently, the device structure has been opti mized to feature a lower bandgap in the top cell and thinner absorbers. This approach addresses the instability issues associated with high bandgap perovskite materials and reduces fabrication costs, particularly for the silicon subcell. At each investigated location, we calculated the

optimization of the bandgap for the perovskite top cell at various absorber thicknesses. This data is crucial for balancing fabrication costs, device stability (lifetime), and output power. The results of this study should provide valuable guidelines for the development of monolithic perovskite-based tandem devices in outdoor applications.
