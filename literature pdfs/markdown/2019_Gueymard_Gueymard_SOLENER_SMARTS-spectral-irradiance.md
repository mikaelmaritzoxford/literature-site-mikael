**2. Historical perspective** ThedevelopmentofSMARTSstartedintheearly1990s(Gueymard,
1993). Originally, version 1 (v1) of the model was intended as an evolution of Bird’s SPCTRAL2 model developed a decade earlier (Bird, 1982a,1984;BirdandRiordan,1986).Comparedtothisearliermodel, a number of improvements were progressively incorporated, and re- sulted in an algorithm that benefited from a newer extraterrestrial (referred to as “air mass zero”, AM0) spectrum, increased spectral re- solution,andrecentatmosphericabsorptiondata.Thefirstapplications of SMARTS were related to the evaluation of daylighting solutions in office buildings and of spectrally-selective glazing systems in high- performance windows (Gueymard, 1995a, 1995b). Successive rounds of development led to versions v2 (Gueymard,
1994), v2.7 (Gueymard, 1995c), v2.8 (Gueymard, 2001), and v2.9.2 (Gueymard,2003).Inparallel,acomprehensivestudywasconductedto obtain reference spectra for the PV industry (Gueymard et al., 2002). The most recent public version, v2.9.8, has been released recently (Gueymard, 2018c) and includes various improvements that are dis- cussed below—in addition to corrections of various coding errors that affected earlier versions.

**3. Modeling principle** Sinceitsinception,theguidingprinciplebehindthedevelopmentof SMARTS has been to offer a good engineering compromise between accuracy and speed of execution. Advanced radiative transfer models that are normally used in atmospheric sciences (e.g., meteorology or remote sensing) generally aim at maintaining highly accurate irra- diancepredictionsunder anycircumstance, andatallverticallevelsof the atmosphere. Examples of such atmospheric models include MODTRAN (Berk et al., 1999), libRadtran (Emde et al., 2016; Mayer and Kylling, 2005), and SBDART (Ricchiazzi et al., 1998). To achieve this accuracy goal under any circumstance, the appropriate resolution of the equations of radiative transfer imposes computer-intensive al- gorithms, and hence large computingpower and long execution times. In sharp contrast, the early solar engineering approaches (Bird, 1984; Leckner, 1978) aimed exclusively at predicting the surface clear-sky irradiance, and attempted to replace the rigorous method mentioned abovebyhighlyparameterizedalgorithmsthatcouldexecuteextremely fast. This, however, also induced considerable simplifications and/or empirical relationships, resulting in loss of both accuracy and gen- eralizability. Fromthestart,SMARTSattemptedtoofferanoptimalcompromise, by providing generalizable results with more detail and precise para- meterizations than in simpler models, while giving the user a lot of flexibility in the input selection process, and still being considerably faster and easier to handle than rigorous physical models. Moreover, the spectral resolution adopted in SMARTS is substantially better than that of earlier simple spectral models. Conclusion** Inthiscontribution,themaincomponentsoftheSMARTSclear-sky radiative transfer model have been discussed, highlighting the recent developmentsinitslatestrelease.Thesedevelopmentswere motivated bytheperceivedappealformoreaccuracyandaugmentedcapabilities, with the desire to better serve a broad range of potential applications spread over many scientific disciplines, as evidenced by a thorough literature survey. Among the new developments highlighted here, a revised extra- terrestrial (AM0) spectrum has been introduced in the present update (v2.9.8)ofthemodel,forananticipatedimprovementintheprediction of spectral irradiance distributions over various wavebands. Another important development is the introduction of new aerosol models for dust and smoke mixtures, allowing the evaluation of the circumsolar irradiance under these, or other, aerosol mixtures. It is found, for in- stance, that the broadband circumsolar irradiance sensed by a pyrhe- liometer remains normally small under aerosols of the rural type, but can be significant (>5% of DNI) in a dusty environment.
