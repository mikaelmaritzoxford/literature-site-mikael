When laterally connecting many of such EQC models, a so-called distributed diode model is achieved. Such a model has been established and used to investigate metal grid related effects for multijunction cells [20], [21]. However, to the knowledge of the authors, there exists no publicly available solar cell simulation tool yet implementing a distributed diode model specifically for tandem solar cells. In this article, we address this lack of simulation capabilities by introducing an extension to the “skin concept” implemented in the solar cell simulation tool Quokka3 [15]. In this concept, the DD equations are solved in 3-D, but only within the quasi-neutral bulk, which typically represents the main part of the silicon wafer. All effects taking place close to the surfaces, i.e., within the “skins,” are lumped into few effective properties forming a boundary condition. This has been shown to be very successful in reducing the required numerical mesh size and computation time, being compatible with large-area 3-D solar cell simulations up to full wafer sizes. We describe how this skin boundary condition is extended to support an equivalent circuit model representing a top cell within a tandem cell. Additionally, an EQC model type for the quasi-neutral bulk is implemented as an option. This offers the user of Quokka3 the flexibility to simulate a 3-D tandem cell either with a coupled 3-D DD bulk and EQC top cell model, or with a simpler distributed diode model. We first describe the details of the model as implemented in Quokka3. Then we validate the model by comparisons against 2-D Sentaurus TCAD [16] simulations. Lastly, the usefulness of the new capabilities is showcased by an investigation of perimeter losses for laboratory tandem solar cells developed at Fraunhofer ISE.

### II. MODEL DESCRIPTION

In the skin concept implemented in Quokka3 the solar cell is split into 3 essential parts: the quasi-neutral bulk, which com- monly represents the main part of the silicon wafer; the laterally conductive skin, which comprises the near-surface region of the bulk material and the functional layers on top; and) the metal- lization grid which contacts the skin and conducts the current to the terminals. In the bulk, the quasi-neutral form of the DD equations are solved in 3-D in steady-state, closed via the lumped electrical properties of the skins as laterally conductive boundary conditions, which was introduced as the “conductive boundary model” [22]. The main lumped electrical properties of a skin are the sheet resistance*R*sheetand the recombination property*J*0skin. These two properties are experimentally well accessible, which rendered this modeling approach very useful and popular next to the conventional way of solving DD also within near-surface re- gion, i.e., not treating the skin as a separate entity. By omitting the numerical mesh within the skin for the 3-D solver, and by being able to use the quasi-neutral simplification of the DD equations, the implementation in Quokka3 is numerically highly efficient

and copes with large-area 3-D domains up to full-size solar cells. The skin concept also enables a so called “multiscale” ap- proach [23]. Here a skin is not defined by its lumped properties, but rather by its detailed properties like e.g., doping profile and surface recombination velocities. First, DD is solved in 1-D for each detailed skin, and from the results the lumped properties are automatically provided as a boundary condition to the subse- quent 3-D bulk solver. These 1-D detailed skin simulations add little computational effort, meaning that the multiscale approach enables large-area 3-D simulation accounting for the detailed properties of the skin, with results equivalent to a full 3-D DD simulation with orders of magnitude higher computational demand. The results are arguably accurate for most wafer-based silicon solar cell simulation tasks, due to the skins being typically much thinner than other cell dimensions causing the imposed assumptions to be valid [15]. In the following, the model extensions are described for the case of a top cell within the front skin, representing,

e.g., the perovskite top cell within a perovskite-silicon tandem cell. The approach however can well be generalized to a subcell being contained in a skin on any side of the device and is equally applicable for other top cell technologies. Notably, the 3-D implementation in Quokka3 so far only solves for steady-state conditions, meaning that it cannot model transient effects, like top cell hysteresis effects.

Notably, the input parameters, i.e., the electrical and optical properties of the bulk and skins, are identical irrespective of the bulk model type. This means that within Quokka3, switching between the 3-D DD and distributed diode model is possible by a single option in the settings. The benefits of the EQC bulk model type are greatly reduced computational demand and additionally increased numerical ro- bustness, e.g., a numerical issue when solving for small applied voltages for an illuminated solar cell, in particular short-circuit conditions, is alleviated with the new bulk model type. However, the drawback is a lack of physical detail, mainly the assumption of a depth-independent Fermi-level split. This results in the inability to resolve current crowding effects at local contacts, nonideal current collection, diffusion-limited recombination, etc. Also, the implementation in Quokka3 strictly allows only a single skin polarity per side, making it incompatible with, e.g., interdigitated back contact solar cells. It thus depends on the concrete solar cell design and simulation task whether the EQC bulk model type is suitable and advantageous. It is in particular useful for metal-grid investigations on large solution domains but should be avoided if speed and robustness are no practical limitation. For tandem cell simulations with an EQC top cell model the EQC bulk model type appears the natural match and is thus the recommended option for high numerical robustness and speed. However, it can be useful or even necessary to use the 3-D DD model type in some cases, e.g., if a detailed optimization of the bottom cell is the task. ### III. MODEL VALIDATION

In order to validate the proposed model and its implementa- tion, we compare tandem simulation results of Quokka3 with Sentaurus TCAD [16], in particular to test the aforementioned simplifications regarding the missing intermediate lateral con- ductance. In Sentaurus, we perform a fullly detailed optoelectrical sim- ulation of a perovskite-silicon tandem cell featuring a 250 *μ*m thick 1 Ωcm p-type cSi bottom cell with an ideal nonrecom- bining full-area rear contact, 1 ms SRH bulk lifetime, and an ideal nonrecombining *n*-type poly-Si front emitter. The top cell consists of a state-of-the-art planar *p-i-n* perovskite layer stack as used in [26] featuring a 20 nm intermediate indium tin oxide (ITO) layer as recombination TCO, a 1nm thin 2PACz layer as hole transport layer, a 450 nm thick perovskite layer, and a C60/SnO2layer stack as ETL. The front TCO features lateral conductance of 150 Ω/. The cell is solved optically via ray-tracing and transfer-matrix-method according to several prior publications [10], [11]. The electrical simulation of the full tandem cell solves the semiconductor equations within the whole 2-D element including the complex thin-film layer stack. For the comparison with Quokka3, *JV* hysteresis due to mobile ions within the perovskite was neglected. We first perform a quasi-1-D simulation of the tandem cell in Sentaurus TCAD. The top cell’s light and dark *JV*-curves are extracted and subsequently fitted by a two-diode model. Notably the two-diode parameters are significantly different between the light and dark case, indicating that the two-diode model should be considered a mathematical parameterization rather than an accurate physical model of the perovskite top cell. We do not want to speculate at this point whether this is something typical for perovskite cells, or particular for the chosen theoretical prop- erties, but it has implications to the following 2-D simulations, see below. In Quokka3 we set the optical generation currents in the sub cell same as in the Sentaurus simulations, assume the same bottom cell bulk properties and zero surface recombination, and apply the two-diode model parameters to the top cell. A close match of the *JV*-curves between Quokka3 and Sentaurus is observed, with the light *JV* parameters deviating less than 0.1%, evidencing that the basic sub cell series connection is correctly modelled in Quokka3. This introduces strong lateral variations in order to reveal any potential influence of laterally conductive layers between the subcells. IV. PERIMETER DESIGN OF 1X1CM² TANDEM CELLS

Most laboratory perovskite-silicon tandem cells are designed via using wafer-size silicon bottom cells and placing small perovskite top cells on it, which are measured via a shadow mask (“designated area” measurement). In particular for highest efficiency devices, the active cell size is around 1 cm x 1 cm or even smaller, to relax requirements on thin-film processing uniformity, and also to avoid a metal grid and thus shading losses within the active cell area. One downside of this design is that the inactive bottom cell perimeter leads to carrier transport losses into the dark perimeter region. As the active cell area to perimeter ratio is rather low for these small sizes, these losses can be substantial, and it is thus a worthwhile effort to design the perimeter region in such way to minimize these losses. Because experimental investigation of various perimeter designs is of substantial effort, not the least due to often moderate reproducibility of laboratory solar cell batches, numerical tandem simulation can effectively support this task. Here, we investigate one particular perimeter design idea, namely employing full-area top cell films, and structuring only the bottom cell emitter and recombination ITO layers to define the small active area. The idea is that the electrical isolation of the subcells within the perimeter region may minimize the perimeter losses, with a relatively straightforward manufacturing process flow, see Fig. 7. We set up a quarter symmetry solution domain of such 1 cm x 1 cm tandem cells in Quokka3, where the illumination is restricted to the active cell area, as in the experimental desig- nated area measurement, see Fig. 8. The required electro-optical inputs are roughly calibrated to the Fraunhofer ISE tandem cells achieving an efficiency of *∼*25% [26], see Table I for details on the input parameters. The only difference between the active and perimeter region besides the illumination is the definition of a “vertical skin resistance,” i.e., the isolation resistance, which is the resistance between the bottom cell bulk and the top-cell.

V. C ONCLUSION
We presented an extension of the “skin-concept” implemented in Quokka3 to support an EQC model of a subcell, which allows steady-state electro-optical 3-D tandem cell simulations with a particular focus on wafer-based c-Si bottom cells. As the extension is contained with the skin boundary condition to the 3-D bulk carrier transport solver, the numerical mesh and computational demand is not largely affected, enabling large-area 3-D solution domains as with single-junction cells. The required inputs are the electro-optical characteristics of the subcell, namely the*JV*-curve, parameterized into a two-diode model, and the spectrally resolved EQE and transmission to the bottom cell. Similar as with the single-junction skin properties *J₀* and *R*sheet, these top cell inputs can be determined from cell- level measurements. This makes the approach agnostic to the top cell technology, and substantially simpler to apply compared to a full 3-D DD tandem model, which requires many hard-to- determine properties of the involved materials and interfaces. Notably, the inputs can also be populated from detailed 1-D DD simulations, resulting in a tandem multiscale model, which is planned to be implemented in Quokka3 in future as well. Also, we presented a new bulk model type in Quokka3, by simplifying the 3-D DD model to an EQC model without a depth discretization. This increases numerical speed and robustness, at the cost of neglecting some 3-D transport related effects in the c-Si cell and can easily be switched by a single setting in Quokka3. If combined with a top cell skin, a quasi-3-D distributed diode model of a tandem cell is created. This appears to be the natural choice for tandem modeling when already using an EQC model for the top cell and is in fact the recommended choice for most simulation tasks. There are however situations for which the distributed diode tandem model in its current implementation can be an oversimplification, e.g., for strong lateral inhomogeneities or perimeter effects. We show this to be related mainly to accounting only for a single carrier type for the bottom cell’s lateral bulk carrier transport and to neglecting intermediate conducting layers. The former is alleviated by using the DD bulk model type. A proper alleviation for both bulk model types requires the addition of two intermediate conducting layers in the numerical mesh, which is planned to be implemented in the future. While not directly applicable to optimize the top cell proper- ties themselves, the proposed model can be useful for many tasks ranging from: understanding basic tandem effects with relatively simple input parameters; accurate and comprehensive metal grid design optimization; detailed bottom cell optimization; quantifi- cation of the impact of lateral inhomogeneities on global cell performance; and investigating cell measurement setups within the simulation, e.g., masked illumination or probe positioning. As an application example we investigated small-area labo- ratory Si-perovskite tandem cells employed on a full-wafer c-Si bottom cell, where the idea was to suppress perimeter losses

by isolating the top and bottom cells in the perimeter region. By respective Quokka3 simulations including the isolated dark perimeter we could show that the idea is in principle promising to suppress the larger part of the perimeter losses. We also found that a front-junction bottom cell design with high emitter sheet resistance is beneficial in any case to suppress minority carrier flow into the perimeter. The application example also highlights the limits of the implemented distributed diode model to quantify such perimeter losses, which can be alleviated by including additional laterally conducting layers in between the subcells.
